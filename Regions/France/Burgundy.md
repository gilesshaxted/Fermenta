# Burgundy

> [!abstract] At a glance
> The benchmark for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, divided into tiny vineyard plots where the exact site matters more than the grape.

> [!info] Quick facts
> - **Country:** [[France]]
> - **Part of:** [[Europe]]
> - **Climate:** Semi-continental
> - **Soils:** limestone, marl, clay
> - **Main grapes:** [[Pinot Noir]], [[Chardonnay]], [[Aligoté]], [[Gamay]]
> - **Styles:** [[Crémant]]

## Landscape and topography
A long, narrow band of vineyards running roughly north to south, mostly on east-facing slopes at about 200 to 400 metres. The Côte d'Or ridge is the core, with Chablis to the north-west and the Mâconnais and Beaujolais to the south.

## Soil and geology
Jurassic limestone and marl, with the mix of clay and limestone shifting from plot to plot. Marl-rich ground tends to suit Chardonnay, while limestone-rich clay tends to suit Pinot Noir.

## Climate
Semi-continental, with cold winters, warm summers, and a real risk of spring frost, hail and rain at harvest. Vintage variation is large.

## History
Monks, especially the Benedictines of Cluny and the Cistercians of Cîteaux, mapped the vineyards over centuries and identified the distinct plots known as climats. Napoleonic inheritance laws divided holdings among heirs, which is why ownership is so fragmented today. The climats of the Côte d'Or were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2015.

## Production and wine styles
Red Burgundy is Pinot Noir and white Burgundy is Chardonnay, with Aligoté and Gamay in supporting roles. The classification rises with the vineyard rather than the producer: regional, village, Premier Cru, then Grand Cru. Growers (domaines) and négociants both bottle wine, so the producer's name matters.

## What to look for
- Learn the ladder: Bourgogne, village, Premier Cru, Grand Cru.
- The producer matters as much as the vineyard.
- Prices climb steeply, so the Mâconnais and Côte Chalonnaise are the value routes in.
